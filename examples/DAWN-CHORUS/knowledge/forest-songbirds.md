# DAWN-CHORUS — Forest Songbirds of Salmonberry Creek

Species accounts for the songbirds of the Cedar Forest, Creek Corridor and forest edges at Salmonberry Creek Preserve: how to recognise them, where and when to find them, and something worth knowing about each. For their songs in detail, see songs-and-calls.md; for confusing pairs, see look-alikes.md.

---

## Wrens and Thrushes

### Pacific Wren

A tiny, round, dark brown bird with a stubby tail often cocked straight up, about 4 inches long and weighing about a third of an ounce. It creeps like a mouse through root wads, fallen logs and sword fern in the Cedar Forest, and sings an enormous bubbling song from a stump. Present year-round. Until 2010 it was lumped with the eastern Winter Wren.

### Swainson's Thrush

A slim brown thrush with a spotted breast and a buffy eye-ring that gives it a spectacled look; birds here have warm russet-brown backs. It skulks in salmonberry and alder along the creek and sings its upward-spiralling flute song at dawn and dusk. Swainson's Thrushes arrive in mid-May, as salmonberries ripen, and leave by late September for Central and South America. Heard far more often than seen.

### Varied Thrush

Robin-sized and striking: orange eyebrow, orange throat and wing bars, slate-blue back and a black band across an orange breast (grey and fainter on females). Feeds on the ground in the Cedar Forest, flipping leaves. Most numerous from October to April, when birds from the mountains and farther north winter in the lowlands; in cold snaps they come to backyards.

### Hermit Thrush

A brown thrush with a spotted breast and a reddish tail that it slowly raises and lowers. At the preserve it is a winter visitor and spring migrant, quiet in the underbrush of the Cedar Forest from October to April; it nests in the mountains. Its song, heard mainly in April, is considered one of the most beautiful in North America.

### American Robin

The familiar grey-backed, orange-breasted thrush of lawns and meadows, present year-round. On lawns and meadows robins run, stop and cock their heads to find worms. In winter they gather in flocks to eat berries such as madrone, holly and mountain ash. Usually the first singer of the dawn chorus.

## Chickadees, Nuthatches and Tiny Treetop Birds

### Chestnut-backed Chickadee

A small chickadee with a sooty brown cap, white cheeks and rich chestnut back and flanks. The common chickadee of the Cedar Forest, feeding high in conifers year-round. It rarely sings a whistled song; listen for its hoarse, fast "chick-a-dee". In winter it often forms the core of mixed flocks with kinglets, nuthatches and creepers.

### Black-capped Chickadee

A small, round bird with a black cap and bib, white cheeks and buffy flanks, common in the alders, willows and around the trailhead year-round. Sings "fee-bee" and calls "chick-a-dee-dee-dee". Chickadees hide thousands of seeds each autumn and remember where they put them.

### Red-breasted Nuthatch

A small, short-tailed bird with a black line through the eye, a white eyebrow, blue-grey back and rusty underparts. It climbs down tree trunks headfirst, which no woodpecker or creeper does. Its nasal "yank-yank" call sounds like a toy trumpet. Present year-round in the conifers.

### Brown Creeper

A tiny, streaky brown bird with a white belly and a thin curved bill, so well camouflaged it looks like a moving piece of bark. It spirals up a tree trunk probing for insects, then flies down to the base of the next tree and starts again. Year-round in the Cedar Forest.

### Golden-crowned Kinglet

One of North America's smallest songbirds, weighing about as much as a nickel: olive-grey with a bold striped face and a yellow crown patch (orange in the centre on males). It hovers at the tips of conifer branches and survives freezing nights by huddling. Its very high "tsee-tsee-tsee" is easy to miss.

### Ruby-crowned Kinglet

A tiny olive bird with a broken white eye-ring and white wing bars that constantly flicks its wings. The male's red crown patch is usually hidden and flashes only when he is excited. Common from October to April in shrubs and alders; in April the males sing a surprisingly loud, bubbling song before leaving to nest in the mountains.

### Bushtit

A tiny, plain grey-brown bird with a long tail, travelling in chattering flocks of 10 to 40 that pour through the shrubs one after another. Pairs weave a hanging, sock-shaped nest up to a foot long from moss, lichen and spider silk. Present year-round along the Creek Loop and trailhead.

## Jays, Towhees and Sparrows

### Steller's Jay

A bold, dark blue jay with a black head and a tall black crest, the state's most common jay in conifer forests. Loud, clever and a gifted mimic, it imitates Red-tailed Hawks and other birds. Steller's Jays bury acorns, hazelnuts and conifer seeds for later. Present year-round, especially around the trailhead picnic tables.

### Spotted Towhee

A large sparrow with a black hood, red eyes, white-spotted black back and rufous sides. It scratches noisily in dry leaves with a two-footed backward hop, and its cat-like "mreee?" call comes from dense brush. Common year-round along the Creek Loop and Meadow Path.

### Dark-eyed Junco

A neat sparrow with a black hood (grey on females), rusty-brown back, pink bill and white outer tail feathers that flash when it flies. The local form is called the "Oregon" junco. Juncos flock on the ground in winter, and some pairs nest on the preserve in spring, often on the ground under ferns.

### Song Sparrow

A streaky brown sparrow with a dark central breast spot; birds in the Puget lowlands are darker and rustier than those elsewhere. Sings from shrubs and fence posts year-round and pumps its tail in flight. One of the most common birds on the preserve, especially along the Marsh Boardwalk.

### Golden-crowned Sparrow

A large, plain-breasted sparrow with a yellow forehead framed in black; winter birds are duller. Present from October to April in flocks along brushy edges and the Meadow Path, often with White-crowned Sparrows. Its "oh dear me" song is a sign of spring migration.

## Warblers

### Wilson's Warbler

A small, bright yellow warbler; males have a neat round black cap. It flits and hovers through willows and salmonberry along the creek catching insects. Present late April to September, wintering in Mexico and Central America.

### Orange-crowned Warbler

A small, plain olive-yellow warbler; the orange crown patch is almost never visible. One of the earliest spring arrivals, singing its descending trill in the alders from early April. A few spend the winter in the lowlands.

### Black-throated Gray Warbler

A crisp black-and-white striped warbler with a black throat and a tiny yellow spot in front of the eye. Sings its buzzy "weezy weezy" song high in bigleaf maples and Douglas-firs from late April to August.

### Townsend's Warbler

A yellow-and-black warbler with a dark cheek patch outlined in yellow. A winter visitor at the preserve from October to April, feeding high in conifers and sometimes visiting suet at the trailhead feeders; it nests in the mountains and farther north.

## Summer Visitors

### Western Tanager

Males are brilliant yellow with a red-orange head and black wings and back; females are olive-yellow with wing bars. Tanagers feed high in conifers and are easy to overlook despite their colours. Present May to August, wintering in Mexico and Central America.

### Black-headed Grosbeak

Males are cinnamon-orange with a black head, black-and-white wings and a big pale bill for cracking seeds; females are streaky brown with a bold white eyebrow. Present May to August in the Creek Corridor. Both males and females sing, and males help incubate the eggs, singing even while sitting on the nest.

### Olive-sided Flycatcher

A stocky, dark flycatcher with a white throat and dark sides that look like an unbuttoned vest. It perches on the very top of tall snags, sallying out to catch flying insects, and sings "quick-three-beers!". Present late May to August at the Snag Overlook. It has declined sharply across North America and is a conservation priority.

### Western Flycatcher

A small olive-and-yellow flycatcher with a teardrop-shaped white eye-ring, found in shady, damp forest along the creek from May to August. It builds a mossy nest on a ledge, root wad or even under a footbridge. Until 2023 it was called the Pacific-slope Flycatcher.

## Crows and Ravens

### American Crow

The all-black bird of towns and fields, smaller than a raven, with a fan-shaped tail and a "caw" call. Crows are highly social and intelligent, remember individual human faces, and gather in large winter roosts at dusk. Crows here were once called Northwestern Crows; in 2020 they were merged with the American Crow.

### Common Raven

Larger than a crow, with a heavy bill, shaggy throat feathers and a wedge-shaped tail in flight. Ravens croak ("gronk") rather than caw, and often soar like hawks. Occasional at the preserve, usually flying over in pairs.
