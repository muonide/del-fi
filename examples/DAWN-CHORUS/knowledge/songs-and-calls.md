# DAWN-CHORUS — Songs and Calls: What Am I Hearing?

Most birds at Salmonberry Creek Preserve are heard long before they are seen. This guide is organised by what a sound is like, with the species that make it. Mnemonics are memory phrases birders use for a song's rhythm. Species details are in forest-songbirds.md, marsh-and-water-birds.md, owls-and-raptors.md and woodpeckers-and-hummingbirds.md.

---

## Whistles and Flutes

### Flute-like song spiralling upward: Swainson's Thrush

A series of breathy, flute-like phrases that spiral up the scale, each rising higher than the last, like a rising whirlpool of notes. Sung at dawn and especially at dusk from mid-May to August along the Creek Loop. Its call is a liquid "whit" or "quit", and on migration nights you can hear its soft "heep" overhead. Often called the salmonberry bird.

### Clear note, then a cascade of flutes: Hermit Thrush

Each phrase starts with one long, pure note followed by a tumbling cascade of flute-like notes, and the next phrase starts on a different pitch. At the preserve mostly heard in spring migration (April); in winter it gives only a low "chuck" call from the underbrush.

### One long, eerie, buzzy whistle: Varied Thrush

A single long whistle on one pitch, with a buzzy or burry quality, that fades away; after a pause it repeats on a different pitch. It sounds like a phone ringing in the forest or someone whistling through a comb. Heard in the Cedar Forest from late winter into spring, often in fog and drizzle.

### Two sweet notes, the second lower ("fee-bee"): Black-capped Chickadee

A clear two-note whistle, "fee-bee", with the second note lower. Sung from January on, mostly by males. Easily imitated, and chickadees will often answer a good whistle.

### Three sad falling notes ("oh dear me"): Golden-crowned Sparrow

Three clear, slow, descending whistles, "oh dear me" or "I'm so tired". A winter visitor from October to April; the song picks up in spring just before the birds leave for Alaska and British Columbia to nest.

## Rich Warbles and Carols

### Cheery carol before dawn ("cheerily cheer-up"): American Robin

A rich, continuous carol of short rising and falling phrases, "cheerily, cheer-up, cheerio". Usually the first bird singing before dawn in spring and the one that starts the dawn chorus.

### A robin that took voice lessons: Black-headed Grosbeak

Long, sweet, fast warbling phrases, like an American Robin but smoother, faster and more musical. Present May to August in the Creek Corridor. Its call is a sharp, squeaky "eek", like a sneaker on a gym floor.

### Hoarse robin-like phrases: Western Tanager

Short phrases like an American Robin's but hoarse and burry, as if the singer has a sore throat. Its call is a distinctive rising "pit-er-ick". Present May to August, high in the conifers.

## Trills, Buzzes and Chatter

### Long, tumbling, bubbling cascade: Pacific Wren

An astonishingly long, rich, bubbling song of trills and tinkling notes that goes on for five to ten seconds, far too big for such a tiny bird. Sung from low stumps and root wads in the Cedar Forest year-round, loudest from February to June. Call: a sharp double "chip-chip".

### Trill that drops at the end: Orange-crowned Warbler

A fast, slightly buzzy trill that weakens and drops in pitch at the end. One of the first spring migrants, singing from early April in the alders.

### Musical trill on one pitch: Dark-eyed Junco

A simple, musical trill on a single pitch, lasting about two seconds. Juncos sing from treetops in spring; in winter they flock on the ground and give sharp "tsick" and smacking "tack" calls.

### Rapid chatter that speeds up: Wilson's Warbler

A rapid, dry chatter of chips that speeds up and often drops at the end, "chi-chi-chi-chet-chet-chet". Present late April to September in willows and salmonberry along the creek.

### Buzzy notes with an accented ending: Black-throated Gray Warbler

A series of buzzy, wheezy notes, "weezy weezy weezy WEE-zee", usually with a change of pitch at the end. Sings high in bigleaf maples and Douglas-firs from late April to August.

### Buzzy trill and a cat-like mew: Spotted Towhee

The song is a simple buzzy trill, sometimes after a few short notes. More often you hear its call: a rising, cat-like "mreee?" from the brush. Scratching sounds in the leaf litter are often a towhee, too.

### Three notes, a buzz and a trill ("maids, maids, maids, put on your tea-kettle"): Song Sparrow

Two to four clear introductory notes, then a buzz and a jumbled trill: "maids, maids, maids, put-on-your-tea-kettle-ettle-ettle". Sung year-round from shrubs and fence posts, especially along the Marsh Boardwalk.

### "Witchety-witchety-witchety" from the cattails: Common Yellowthroat

A loud, rolling "witchety-witchety-witchety" from low in the cattails and sedges, May to August. The singer is a small yellow warbler with a black mask.

### Very high, thin "tsee-tsee-tsee": Golden-crowned Kinglet and Brown Creeper

A thin, very high "tsee-tsee-tsee" from the conifers is usually a Golden-crowned Kinglet; many people over 50 can no longer hear it. A similar high, thin, wandering song, "trees, trees, see the trees", comes from Brown Creepers spiralling up tree trunks.

## Marsh Sounds

### "Conk-la-ree!": Red-winged Blackbird

A liquid gurgle ending in a rattling trill, "conk-la-reeee", from males displaying their red shoulder patches on the cattails. Starts in February, when the males return to claim territories.

### Gurgling, rattling song from the cattails: Marsh Wren

A dry rattle and gurgle, like a sewing machine or a mechanical toy, from deep in the cattails, day and night from April to August.

### Pig-like grunts from the marsh: Virginia Rail

A series of grunting "oink" notes that descends and speeds up, and a metallic "kid-ick, kid-ick". Rails are rarely seen; the Beaver Pond Blind is the place to listen at dawn.

### A descending whinny: Sora

A high, descending whinny like a tiny horse, and a rising "ker-wee?". Sora are small, secretive rails of the cattails, present April to September.

### Rattle over the water: Belted Kingfisher

A loud, dry, mechanical rattle given in flight over the Beaver Pond and the creek. Heard year-round.

### Deep croak ("frahnk"): Great Blue Heron

A deep, harsh croak, "frahnk", often given when a heron is startled into flight. The heron colony is noisy with clacking bills and chick calls in May and June.

## Hoots and Night Sounds

### "Who cooks for you? Who cooks for you-all?": Barred Owl

Eight or nine rhythmic hoots, "who-cooks-for-you, who-cooks-for-you-all", sometimes ending in a drawl. Pairs also break into wild caterwauling duets. Heard year-round, especially at dusk on the Cedar Trail.

### Deep, soft hoots in a rhythm: Great Horned Owl

Four or five deep, soft hoots, "hoo-h'HOO-hoo-hoo", lower than a Barred Owl. Pairs duet in December and January, when they begin nesting.

### A bouncing ball: Western Screech-Owl

A series of short, hollow hoots that speed up like a ping-pong ball bouncing to a stop. A small owl with ear tufts; heard at night year-round near the creek.

### A backing-up truck at night: Northern Saw-whet Owl

A steady, monotonous series of whistled toots, about two per second, going on for minutes, like a truck backing up. Most often heard from February to April.

## Knocks, Screams and Other Sounds

### Loud drumming and tapping: woodpeckers

Drumming is a woodpecker's song: rapid hammering on a resonant branch, snag or even a metal roof. A slow, loud, powerful drum that trails off is a Pileated Woodpecker. An irregular drum that starts fast and slows into separate taps, like Morse code, is a Red-breasted Sapsucker. Steady, soft tapping while feeding is foraging, not drumming. See woodpeckers-and-hummingbirds.md.

### Wild laughing "wuk-wuk-wuk" in the forest: Pileated Woodpecker or Northern Flicker

A loud, irregular, jungle-like "wuk-wuk-wuk-wuk" is a Pileated Woodpecker. A long, even, rapid "wick-wick-wick-wick" and a single loud "kleer!" is a Northern Flicker.

### A hawk scream ("keee-arr"): Red-tailed Hawk, or a Steller's Jay faking it

The hoarse, descending scream "keee-arrr" is a Red-tailed Hawk, the sound Hollywood uses for eagles. But Steller's Jays imitate it perfectly, so a hawk scream from inside the forest is often a jay. Real Bald Eagles make surprisingly weak, high chittering calls.

### Harsh "shook-shook-shook": Steller's Jay

A loud, harsh "shook-shook-shook" or "shaack", plus clicks, rattles and hawk imitations. The black-crested blue jay of the forest and the trailhead picnic tables.

### A tin horn ("yank-yank"): Red-breasted Nuthatch

A nasal, toy-trumpet "yank-yank-yank", repeated. Heard year-round in the conifers, often from a bird creeping headfirst down a trunk.

### Constant soft "tsit" notes from a moving flock: Bushtit

A flock of 10 to 40 tiny grey birds keeping in touch with soft "tsit" and "sre-e-e" notes as they pour through the shrubs. A sudden shrill trill from the whole flock means a hawk is near.

### "Quick! Three beers!": Olive-sided Flycatcher

A loud, whistled "quick-THREE-BEERS!", the middle note highest, from the top of a tall snag. Late May to August at the Snag Overlook.

### A rising "pseet" in the shady forest: Western Flycatcher

A thin, rising "pseet" or "suweeet", repeated, from the shady creek forest in May to August. Until 2023 this bird was called the Pacific-slope Flycatcher.

### Scratchy squeaks and a loud "pop" from the sky: Anna's Hummingbird

The male's song is a scratchy, squeaky jumble given year-round, even in winter. In a courtship dive he climbs up to 100 feet and swoops down past a female, ending in a loud "pop" made by his tail feathers.

### "Chick-a-dee-dee-dee": chickadees

The familiar "chick-a-dee-dee-dee" call. More "dee" notes mean a more dangerous predator nearby. A hoarser, higher, faster version is the Chestnut-backed Chickadee, the common chickadee of the Cedar Forest.
