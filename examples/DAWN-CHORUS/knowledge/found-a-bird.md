# DAWN-CHORUS — Found a Baby or Injured Bird?

What to do if you find a baby bird on the ground, an injured bird or a bird that hit a window, at Salmonberry Creek Preserve or at home. Most "orphaned" baby birds are not orphans. When in doubt, call a licensed wildlife rehabilitator before doing anything.

---

## Baby Birds

### Fluffy Baby Bird Hopping on the Ground: a Fledgling

If it has feathers, a stubby tail and can hop or flutter, it is a fledgling. This is normal: fledglings leave the nest before they can fly well and spend several days on or near the ground while their parents feed them, often out of sight. Leave it where it is, keep people, dogs and cats away, and watch from a distance; a parent usually visits within an hour. Only if it is in immediate danger, move it a few feet into a nearby shrub.

### Naked or Barely Feathered Baby Bird: a Nestling

A bird with little or no feathers and closed or half-open eyes is a nestling that has fallen or been pushed out. If you can see the nest and reach it safely, put the bird back; parents do not reject young that humans have touched, because most birds have a poor sense of smell. If the nest is gone, make a substitute from a small basket or margarine tub with drainage holes, lined with dry grass, fasten it in the same tree, and watch from far away for an hour. If no parent returns, call a wildlife rehabilitator.

### Ducklings and Goslings

Young ducks and geese leave the nest within a day of hatching and follow a parent to water. A lone duckling can sometimes be reunited with its family if they are in sight; otherwise call a rehabilitator. Never chase a mother duck and her brood, and give the Canada Geese on the Marsh Boardwalk plenty of room in April and May.

## Injured Birds

### Injured Bird

Gently cover the bird with a light towel and place it in a cardboard box with air holes and a soft cloth on the bottom. Keep it in a dark, quiet, warm place away from pets and children, and do not open the box to check on it. Do not give it food or water: the wrong food or water in the airway can kill it. Call a licensed wildlife rehabilitator as soon as possible. Wash your hands afterwards.

### Bird That Hit a Window

A stunned bird may recover on its own. Put it in a closed, ventilated cardboard box in a dark, quiet room, then after one to two hours take the box outside, open it and let the bird fly off. If it cannot fly or seems injured, call a rehabilitator. To prevent collisions at home, break up window reflections with dots or stripes spaced no more than 2 inches apart, outside screens, or external blinds.

### Caught by a Cat

A bird caught by a cat needs a rehabilitator even if it looks unhurt: cat saliva carries bacteria that cause fatal infections within a day or two without antibiotics. Keep cats indoors, especially in the nesting season from April to July.

## Getting Help

### Finding a Wildlife Rehabilitator

Wildlife rehabilitators are licensed to care for injured and orphaned wild animals. The preserve stewards keep the number of the nearest wildlife rehabilitation centre on the trailhead kiosk; replace this line with your local centre's name and phone number. In the United States, Animal Help Now (ahnow.org) finds the nearest help from your location.

### Birds and the Law

Almost all native wild birds, their nests, eggs and feathers are protected in the United States by the Migratory Bird Treaty Act and state law. It is illegal to keep or raise a wild bird yourself, even with good intentions; a licensed rehabilitator can give it the best chance of returning to the wild.
