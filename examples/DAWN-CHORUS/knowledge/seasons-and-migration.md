# DAWN-CHORUS — Seasons and Migration at Salmonberry Creek

What to expect at Salmonberry Creek Preserve through the year: which birds arrive and leave when, when they nest, and when to hear the dawn chorus. Dates are typical for the Puget Sound lowlands and can shift by a week or two with the weather. For live detections, ask the node about new arrivals or send !data; see birdnet-station.md.

---

## The Dawn Chorus

### What the Dawn Chorus Is

The dawn chorus is the burst of birdsong that starts before sunrise, when many birds sing at once to defend territories and attract mates. Birds may sing most at dawn because the still, cool air carries sound well and it is still too dark to feed efficiently. At the preserve the chorus is richest from late April to late June.

### When and Where to Hear It

Arrive 45 minutes before sunrise. The first singers are usually American Robins, followed by Pacific Wrens, Song Sparrows, Spotted Towhees and, from mid-May, Swainson's Thrushes. The Creek Loop at Alder Bend and the Marsh Boardwalk are the best spots. By mid-morning the chorus fades; Swainson's Thrushes sing again at dusk. Ask the node "how was the dawn chorus this morning?" for the listening station's count of species heard this morning.

## Month by Month

### January and February

The quietest months, but not silent. Great Horned Owls call and nest in January; Pacific Wrens and Anna's Hummingbirds sing on mild days. Winter ducks (Bufflehead, Ring-necked Duck, Hooded Merganser) fill the Beaver Pond. Red-winged Blackbirds return to the marsh in February, herons return to the colony, and the first Rufous Hummingbirds arrive in late February as red-flowering currant blooms.

### March

Spring migration begins. Tree Swallows and Violet-green Swallows return to hawk over the pond. Northern Saw-whet Owls toot at night, woodpeckers drum, and Varied Thrushes sing their eerie whistles in the Cedar Forest before most move up to the mountains. The Snag Overlook closes on March 15 for nesting Ospreys.

### April

The arrivals quicken: Orange-crowned Warblers first, then Ospreys, Barn Swallows, Common Yellowthroats and, by late April, Wilson's and Black-throated Gray Warblers. Hermit Thrushes and Ruby-crowned Kinglets sing before leaving for the mountains. Golden-crowned Sparrows sing "oh dear me" and depart for the north. Wood Ducks and Canada Geese are nesting.

### May

The peak month. Black-headed Grosbeaks, Western Tanagers and Western Flycatchers arrive in early May, and Swainson's Thrushes arrive in mid-May: the first one of the year usually sings between May 10 and May 20, as salmonberries ripen. Olive-sided Flycatchers arrive in late May. The dawn chorus is at its fullest, and heron chicks are loud in the colony.

### June and July

The nesting season. Most songbirds are feeding young, and fledglings appear everywhere: short-tailed, fluffy and often on the ground. That is normal; see found-a-bird.md. Song slows through July. Wood Duck and merganser broods cruise the Beaver Pond. Rufous Hummingbird males start leaving in late June.

### August

Quiet and moulting: many birds replace their feathers and hide. Swallows gather on snags before leaving, and the first southbound migrants, such as Wilson's Warblers and Western Tanagers, pass through. The Osprey family leaves the snag.

### September and October

Autumn migration. Swainson's Thrushes and the last warblers leave by late September; Varied Thrushes, Golden-crowned and Fox Sparrows, Hermit Thrushes, Ruby-crowned Kinglets and Townsend's Warblers arrive for the winter in late September and October. Merlins and Sharp-shinned Hawks hunt the migrants. Winter ducks return to the Beaver Pond in October.

### November and December

Winter flocks: chickadees, kinglets, nuthatches, Bushtits and creepers travel together through the forest, and juncos and sparrows flock along the edges. Anna's Hummingbirds stay all winter and depend on feeders in freezing weather. The Christmas Bird Count, a century-old census run by the Audubon Society, takes place between December 14 and January 5.

## Migration

### How Migration Works

Most songbirds migrate at night, navigating by the stars, the Earth's magnetic field and landmarks, and rest and refuel by day. On good migration nights in May and September, the listening station sometimes records the flight calls of thrushes and sparrows passing overhead in the dark. A tiny Rufous Hummingbird may fly from Mexico to Alaska and back each year.

### Who Stays and Who Goes

Year-round residents include Pacific Wren, both chickadees, Red-breasted Nuthatch, Brown Creeper, Steller's Jay, Spotted Towhee, Song Sparrow, Anna's Hummingbird, all the woodpeckers, Barred and Great Horned Owls, Bald Eagle and Great Blue Heron. Summer visitors that nest here and winter to the south include Swainson's Thrush, most of the warblers, Western Tanager, Black-headed Grosbeak, the flycatchers, the swallows, Osprey and Rufous Hummingbird. Winter visitors that nest farther north or in the mountains include Varied Thrush (mostly), Hermit Thrush, Golden-crowned and Fox Sparrows, Townsend's Warbler, Ruby-crowned Kinglet and most of the diving ducks.
